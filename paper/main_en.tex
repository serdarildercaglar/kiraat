\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We present \textsc{Kiraat}, a Turkish read-speech corpus comprising
1,840,404 clips and 3,105.7 hours, extracted from 2,680 audiobook and
spoken-article recordings across 27 channels. The pipeline transcribes
long-form audio, obtains word-level forced alignments, and cuts at sentence
boundaries inferred from the transcript. Quality measurements are retained
as columns for all generated clips; a separate versioned policy selects a
recommended subset of 1,547,494 clips and 2,575.2 hours without deleting
the remaining clips from the release.

A comparison on 206 recordings from all 27 channels reports lowercase-start
rates of 1.39\% for sentence-aligned segmentation and 14.31\% for a VAD-based
baseline. The corresponding unflagged lowercase-start rates are 0.00\% and
12.81\%. These transcript proxies do not establish acoustic boundary
accuracy. Across the recommended subset, 23 clips have lowercase starts.
Exploratory listening audits informed policy revision: all 25 clips in a
clipping-ratio audit were judged usable, but only seven exceeded the tested
threshold; a word-confidence threshold would exclude 15 of 30 audited clips,
14 of which had correct transcripts.

We report quality distributions, 90 inferred speaker clusters, recording-level
training and evaluation partitions with no shared source identifiers or exact normalized
transcripts between training and evaluation, and stage throughput on an
RTX 3090. Speaker identities and corpus-wide transcription accuracy have not
been independently validated, and no downstream TTS experiment is reported.
The contribution is a corpus construction and documentation workflow with
auditable selection decisions, rather than demonstrated synthesis improvement.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Read speech is a useful source of paired audio and text for text-to-speech
(TTS), but recordings collected for speech recognition do not automatically
satisfy synthesis-oriented requirements for sentence boundaries, speaker
consistency, text coverage, and acoustic quality. Turkish resources include
the recognition-oriented Turkish Speech Corpus~\cite{turkicasr}. The question
here is how to segment and document long-form Turkish readings for potential
use in synthesis, rather than whether recognition data exist.

We investigate two questions: how do sentence-based and VAD-based segmentation
differ on the same recordings under transcript-based boundary checks, and how
can quality selection remain auditable while preserving generated clips?
\textsc{Kiraat} combines an audiobook and spoken-article corpus with per-clip
measurements and a versioned selection policy. We evaluate corpus properties
and implementation behavior. Transfer to other languages and improvements in
TTS quality remain questions for subsequent experiments.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Corpus and documentation.} 1,840,404 clips, 3,105.7 hours,
27 channels and 90 inferred speaker clusters, with per-clip measurements
and explicit selection decisions (Section~\ref{sec:corpus}).
\item \textbf{Segmentation comparison.} Sentence-based and implemented
VAD-based strategies are compared on shared recordings and word timestamps,
using explicit transcript-boundary proxies (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).
\item \textbf{Versioned selection.} Measurement stages cannot write
recommendation decisions; a policy selects a subset without deleting the
remaining generated clips (Section~\ref{sec:policy}).
\item \textbf{Exploratory audits.} Small score-stratified listening samples
inform threshold revision, with their interpretation restricted to the audited
items (Section~\ref{sec:negative}).
\item \textbf{Long-form implementation.} Windowed forced alignment and a
CPU/GPU scheduler support multi-hour recordings; stage throughput is reported
(Section~\ref{sec:cost}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Construction pipelines.}
Emilia~\cite{emilia,emilialarge} combines standardization, source separation,
speaker diarization, VAD segmentation, transcription and quality filtering.
GigaSpeech~2~\cite{gigaspeech2} combines automated collection, Whisper-based
transcription and alignment with label refinement for speech recognition.
WhisperX~\cite{whisperx} also separates transcription from forced alignment.
Our pipeline uses established components, including Whisper~\cite{whisper}
and MMS~\cite{mms}; we do not claim these components as new methods.

\paragraph{Segmentation.}
Sentence-oriented cutting is established in LibriTTS~\cite{libritts},
Libriheavy~\cite{libriheavy} and GigaSpeech~\cite{gigaspeech}.
WenetSpeech4TTS~\cite{wenetspeech4tts} revises boundaries and addresses
speaker mixing when adapting an ASR corpus for synthesis. Our contribution
is a comparison on Turkish recordings with shared timestamps and explicit
flags for exceptional cuts. It is not a claim that controlled segmentation
analysis is absent from the literature.

\paragraph{Selection and restoration.}
Emilia retains clips with DNSMOS P.835 OVRL above 3.0; its thresholds can be
adjusted for different uses~\cite{emilia}. WenetSpeech4TTS releases quality
subsets, while LibriTTS-R~\cite{librittsr} restores audio while retaining the
same constituent samples. We retain generated clips across four partitions
and separate their measurements from a versioned recommended-subset policy.
This supports alternative selection over stored measurements, subject to
screening and missingness. No universal superiority over filtering or
restoration is established.

\paragraph{Turkish resources.}
Publicly documented Turkish speech resources were designed primarily for
recognition. Common Voice~\cite{commonvoice} is a multilingual corpus, including
Turkish, whose recordings are both collected and validated by crowdsourcing,
and which its authors describe as public domain. FLEURS~\cite{fleurs} provides
approximately 12 hours per language of read FLoRes-101 sentences under
CC BY 4.0, as a multilingual evaluation benchmark. MediaSpeech~\cite{mediaspeech}
is a manually transcribed ASR evaluation set collected from official media
YouTube channels in Turkish and three other languages. The Turkish Speech
Corpus was introduced for multilingual Turkic speech recognition~\cite{turkicasr}.
These resources differ from \textsc{Kiraat} in recording style and validation:
short prompted sentences or broadcast speech with human or crowd
verification, as opposed to long-form audiobook and spoken-article readings
with automatic transcripts and no human transcript verification. Access also
differs: \textsc{Kiraat} is gated and declares no license (Section~\ref{sec:ethics}).
We distinguish total transcribed speech from independently validated
synthesis training material: neither dataset size nor an ASR label alone
establishes TTS suitability.

\section{Source material and its measured limits}
\label{sec:corpus}
The recorded inventory comprises 2,700 files from 27 public Turkish audiobook
and spoken-article channels: 2,698 readable audio files, one corrupt m4a and
one README, totaling 3,440.2 hours and 200.1\,GB. The run database contains
2,699 source entries, one per audio file. Of these, 2,680 contribute clips;
one (the corrupt m4a) fails decoding, and 18 decode but receive no ASR words
and hence no clips (34.8 source hours; 15 of the 18 from one channel). A
per-source ledger derived from the run database lists source ID, channel,
file name, duration, status and clip count for all 2,699 entries
(\col{docs/source\_ledger.csv}). The run database records 3,430.5 hours for
the 2,698 decoded files; the 9.7-hour difference from the inventory total
reflects separately measured durations and is not reconciled file by file.

\paragraph{Encoding and bandwidth.}
The inventory reports 2,618 AAC files (3,379.7 hours) and 80 MP3 files
(60.5 hours), with 2,693 files at 44.1\,kHz. Bit-rate percentiles are
128/129/130\,kb/s (p05/p50/p95). Bandwidth was probed on one 60-second
excerpt per channel using a 32k-point FFT. The reported median cutoff is
15.7\,kHz (range 13.0--15.8); median energy above 12\,kHz is 0.05\%.
These 27 excerpts inform the choice of a 24\,kHz release rate, but do not
establish bandwidth for every recording or the optimal sampling rate for TTS.

\paragraph{Concentration.}
The largest two channels contribute approximately 35\% of raw hours. No
per-channel cap is applied. Each clip retains channel and duration metadata
so consumers can study or rebalance concentration. In the recommended subset,
the two largest channels account for 38.2\% of hours.

\subsection{Sentence-oriented segmentation}
ASR runs over speech windows and outputs words and timestamps. MMS forced
alignment refines word times; the transcript is divided into sentences, and
audio cuts follow their word spans. VAD supplies ASR regions and clip quality
measurements, but does not define the primary sentence boundaries.

The configuration uses a 1.5\,s minimum clip duration, a 7.0\,s joining
target, a 15.0\,s ceiling, a 1.2\,s maximum joining gap, and leading/trailing
padding of 0.15/0.25\,s. Exceptional intra-sentence cuts receive
\col{forced\_split} or \col{gap\_split} flags. Boilerplate mining uses
repeated channel-level word $n$-grams, separately marking announcements.
These flags are construction annotations, not human judgments of boundary
correctness.

The aligner forms approximately 30\,s windows, with a 60\,s maximum and
0.5\,s padding, using coarse ASR timestamps. A window reader loads and
resamples only the required interval, bounding the alignment waveform and
lattice size. This does not establish constant memory use for all stages.

\subsection{Acoustic boundary refinement}
An energy-based search near an inter-clip word boundary adjusts the cut
within a configured window to reduce phoneme truncation. The recorded
post-refinement listening audits found no truncated syllables in 28 clips
and no truncated words in another 30 clips (20 suspected cases and 10
controls, shuffled). These selected samples do not establish a corpus-wide
acoustic error rate. Listener counts, expertise, clip identifiers, annotation
instructions and per-item labels are not part of this release, so these
audits cannot be independently re-scored.

\subsection{Duplicate heuristic}
Separate readings of the same text are retained for prosodic diversity.
The implemented heuristic groups clips by Turkish-aware normalized text and
exact equality of duration, LUFS, RMS and peak; channel is not in the key.
Matching summary statistics do not prove waveform identity, and re-encoding
or shifted boundaries can prevent true duplicates from matching. The
heuristic flags 576 clips. Clips with missing fingerprint fields bypass the
check. Its precision and recall have not been independently established.

\subsection{Audio preparation and quality columns}
Preparation converts audio to mono, applies a 40\,Hz high-pass filter and
resamples to at most 24\,kHz, without upsampling lower-rate sources at this
stage. Export declares 24\,kHz audio for dataset loading. These operations
modify the waveform; no loudness normalization or peak limiting is applied.
Source separation is used for measurement, and its output is discarded.

Clip measurements include VAD speech ratio and silence, peak/RMS/LUFS,
clipping ratio, DNSMOS P.835 SIG/BAK/OVRL~\cite{dnsmos}, word scores,
and speaker embeddings. DNSMOS is a model-derived quality estimate, not a
human MOS collected for this corpus. Acoustic properties and quality scores
are retained even when the selection policy rejects a clip.

\paragraph{Music screening.}
The AST AudioSet model~\cite{ast} scores music labels for every clip.
HDemucs~\cite{hdemucs} runs only when that score is at least 0.05.
Separation was skipped for 1,455,201 clips and performed for 385,203
clips in the stored run. Skipped clips receive the sentinel $-80$\,dB in
\col{music\_to\_speech\_db}, and
\col{music\_db\_separated} is false. This sentinel does not demonstrate absent
music. For separated clips the column estimates accompaniment-to-vocal energy,
not calibrated speech/noise level or perceptual audibility. A policy requiring
separation below the screening threshold needs further model inference.

\paragraph{Text fields and confidence.}
\col{text\_raw} joins ASR words; \col{text} applies light cleaning;
\col{text\_spoken} expands numerals and abbreviations. Per-operation transformation logs are not exported. The last field is intended for TTS training,
but normalization has not been validated corpus-wide against human transcripts.
\col{word\_confidence} is the minimum word score, using ASR scores in the
current configuration; forced-alignment mean/minimum scores are separate.
Neither is a calibrated probability of transcript correctness.

\section{The recommended subset policy}
\label{sec:policy}
The stage base class rejects clip-stage outputs containing \col{recommended}
or \col{decision}. Operational thresholds are specified in configuration;
implementation helpers also contain defaults. The configuration loader rejects
unknown sections and keys. The column table is generated from the schema,
while narrative documentation and remote cards require separate checks.

Version~6 requires \col{speech\_ratio} $\ge 0.60$,
\col{internal\_silence\_sec} $\le 1.0$, and absence of
\col{oversize}, \col{forced\_split}, \col{gap\_split}, \col{short},
\col{duplicate}, \col{boilerplate} and \col{background\_music} flags.
The result is 1,547,494 clips and 2,575.2 hours: 84.1\% of generated clips
and 82.9\% of their duration. Exclusion reasons and policy version are
retained. Missing required policy measurements fail the applicable rule.

All generated clips remain in the release. This guarantee begins after
segmentation; it does not mean that every source interval or failed decoding
result becomes a clip. Alternative selection is possible over stored
measurements, but missing or screened values limit policies that can be
recomputed without inference.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Segmentation comparison}
\label{sec:ablation}
The research notebook reports a comparison on 206 recordings from all
27 channels. The sentence arm is read from the full-run database; the VAD
arm is constructed from speech regions on the same recordings and shared
word timestamps, using the same configuration object. Source selection uses
seed 20260906 and up to eight recordings per channel. Coverage of all
channels does not imply equal audio duration per channel.

The arms also differ in grouping, exceptional-cut flags, boilerplate handling,
and boundary refinement. In particular, the VAD implementation does not use
the sentence joining target in the same way. This is a strategy comparison,
not an isolated intervention on a single cut point or a benchmark against
executed Emilia or LibriTTS pipelines.

We define \emph{lowercase start} using the Turkish-aware \col{is\_lower\_start}
routine (the first alphabetic character after leading punctuation is tested;
an initial numeral is not flagged). A second proxy tests absence of
sentence-final punctuation after stripping closing quotes/brackets. These
proxies depend on ASR casing and punctuation, which also guide sentence
segmentation. Consequently they are not independent labels of acoustic
truncation or sentence integrity.

The reported lowercase-start rate is 1.39\% for sentence segmentation and
14.31\% for the implemented VAD baseline. Among all clips, unflagged
lowercase-start rates are 0.00\% and 12.81\%, respectively. ``Unflagged''
means an empty flag list, although the arms do not have identical flag
vocabularies. The metadata lack a dedicated flag for many proxy-positive
baseline clips; their text still permits the proxy to be computed. They
should therefore not be described as undetectable.

The sentence arm sums to 240.54 clip-hours and the VAD arm to 254.18.
These are padded clip-duration totals, not measured unique source coverage;
VAD padding can overlap between adjacent clips. Retained speech coverage,
overlap and omitted words must be reported before interpreting the difference
as yield. The values are taken from the research notebook. The per-source
outputs of the baseline arm are not part of this release, so the comparison
cannot be re-derived from the released database alone; the implementation
is \col{scripts/ablate\_segmentation.py}.

Mean alignment scores are 0.9442 and 0.9459. Mean per-clip minimum scores
are 0.7319 and 0.6469, but the arms have different word-count and duration
distributions. Longer clips have more opportunities for a low minimum, so
this statistic alone does not establish poorer edge alignment. No paired
recording-level confidence interval or significance test is reported.

\subsection{Exploratory listening audits}
\label{sec:negative}
The documented procedure hides channel and measurement values, samples across
score bands, shuffles clips and records judgments before revealing scores.
The notebook describes six policy/refinement rounds: two on music, two on
boundaries, one on clipping and one on word confidence. The exact listener
count, inter-rater agreement and per-item labels have not been archived with the available source materials. We therefore treat these as exploratory audits, not
population estimates of error or training utility.

\paragraph{Clipping ratio.}
A candidate ceiling of 0.002 excluded 536 clips in a development sample;
532 were from one channel. These are not full-release exclusion counts.
Among 25 audited clips across six score bands (0--0.0227), 24 were judged
free of audible distortion and one slightly distorted; all were judged usable.
Only seven exceeded the ceiling, and none of those seven was judged audibly
distorted. This motivated removal of the candidate rule in policy v5.
It does not establish that clipping is harmless in general.

\paragraph{ASR word confidence.}
Thirty audited clips span six bands from 0.18 to 0.99. Twenty-nine transcripts
were judged verbatim correct and one had a small error. A minimum confidence
of 0.60 would exclude 15 clips, 14 of which had correct transcripts. The rule
was removed in v6. The reported rank correlation with alignment score is
$+0.262$, but agreement between two automatic scores is not independent
validation of transcript accuracy. This sample cannot supply corpus WER/CER.

\paragraph{Music.}
An initial audit found clearly audible music in 6/6 clips in the
$-40$ to $-33$\,dB band. A later 36-clip audit, sampled across four bands,
reported audible music in 86\% of flagged clips and no music in 11/14
unflagged clips. These stratified observations are not prevalence-weighted
precision or recall. The current flag requires both separated level above
$-40$\,dB and AudioSet score $\ge 0.3$. The notebook reports Spearman
$+0.479$ between the scores on 3,097 separated clips; this screened sample
does not characterize clips for which separation was skipped.

\paragraph{DNSMOS.}
No DNSMOS threshold is used in the policy. Across the whole corpus,
83.57\% of clips have OVRL $\ge 3.0$. This describes the effect of one
hypothetical threshold, not its validity for Turkish synthesis data.

\subsection{Inferred speaker clusters}
ECAPA-TDNN~\cite{ecapa} embeds up to 12 deterministically selected clips of
at least 3\,s per recording. The stage exports a normalized centroid and
within-recording clip-pair cosine statistics. Recordings with fewer than two
eligible clips do not receive a centroid; this is not speaker diarization.

Average-linkage clustering uses a similarity threshold derived from two
proxy distributions: per-recording within-clip mean similarity, and centroid
similarity between different channels (up to 200,000 sampled pairs, seed
20260907). The code equalizes their empirical tail rates. These proxies are
not labeled same-speaker and different-speaker pairs: one recording can
contain multiple voices, and one narrator can occur on different channels.
They also aggregate different types of similarity observations.

The notebook reports threshold 0.532, proxy tail error 0.52\%, and 90
clusters over 2,677 recordings with embeddings; 21 readable recordings lack
an embedding. The 0.52\% is not a speaker-verification EER measured against
known identities. Median cluster margin is reported as 0.445; centroids
include the recording itself, so positive margins are not independent
validation. The resulting cluster label is released per clip as
\col{speaker\_id}, but the recording embeddings are not part of this release,
so the clustering cannot be recomputed from released files. No labeled
same/different-speaker pairs have been audited.

The reported assignment has more than one cluster in 13 channels and nine
clusters spanning several channels. These are inferred relationships, not
verified narrator identities. The largest cluster contains 18.2\% of hours,
the top five 54.4\%, and the top ten 75.0\%; 17 clusters have less than one
hour. Speaker-ID metadata should be treated as provisional clustering labels.

The remaining 316,733 clips are in \col{rest}: 292,910 non-recommended clips
and 23,823 recommended clips unused after quota trimming or text cleanup.
Thus recordings can appear in \col{rest} and one of train/dev/test.
Only the three named training/evaluation partitions have disjoint source IDs.

Before cleanup, 2,263 test clips and 2,723 dev clips shared normalized text
with training. Evaluation clips with these keys are moved to \col{rest}.
The normalization applies Unicode NFC, Turkish-aware lowercasing, punctuation
removal and whitespace collapsing. After cleanup, no train--dev or train--test
key overlaps remain. This does not exclude near-duplicate text, shared books,
re-uploads under distinct source IDs or pretraining contamination. A separate database audit finds 12 shared normalized text keys between
dev and test. These pairs do not share source IDs, but their text is not
independent.

The reported cluster counts are 89 in train and 26 each in dev/test, with
25 of 26 test clusters also present in train. This is predominantly a
seen-cluster evaluation, subject to cluster-label uncertainty. Consumers
expanding training with \col{rest} must exclude every dev/test source ID and
recheck text overlap. Changing the selection policy does not preserve these
partition guarantees automatically.

\section{Throughput and reproducibility}
\label{sec:cost}
The notebook reports processing on an RTX 3090 (24\,GB), with 12 CPU
workers and a 280\,W GPU power cap. Reported stage rates are 94 audio-hours
per hour for alignment, 46 clips/s for QC, 28 clips/s for music (two GPU
workers on this machine), 81.1 clips/s for DNSMOS, and approximately one
second per recording for speaker embeddings. They are stage measurements,
not an end-to-end runtime or measured energy budget. CPU model, host-memory
peak, exact profiling inputs and total wall time remain undocumented here.
The notebook reports approximately 567\,GB of working storage, including
284\,GB of prepared audio and 246\,GB of clips; these intermediate files
are not included in the metric-database archive.

The export implementation records git state, stage versions, configuration,
package versions and model identifiers in \col{run.json}. However, the speaker
loader does not pass the configured revision, and the provenance model/package
lists omit the speaker model, SpeechBrain and scikit-learn. Consequently
complete weight pinning cannot be claimed. The original run record,
per-source ablation outputs, speaker embeddings and listening labels are not
part of this release; the source ledger is. A commit reference alone is
insufficient when the run used uncommitted changes.

The stored full-run database can be checked with
\col{scripts/verify\_card.py}. The 150 implemented
checks pass on the stored database, including regenerated duplicate flags, policy decisions and
partition counts. This verifies consistency of stored results with the card,
not waveform integrity, human transcript accuracy, speaker labels or the
missing baseline outputs. Models and the full pipeline were not rerun.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
\paragraph{No downstream synthesis evaluation.}
No TTS model trained on this corpus is evaluated. Claims concern construction
and documentation, not improved intelligibility, naturalness or voice similarity.
An equal-budget comparison of corpus selections would test utility in a
future model study; it is not needed to report a clearly scoped corpus.

\paragraph{Automatic transcripts and alignments.}
WER/CER against an independently transcribed representative sample is absent.
Casing/punctuation checks depend on the same ASR output used for segmentation.
Numerals fall back to ASR timing when the aligner cannot represent them; a
separate spoken-form mapping could preserve the published text, but has not
been evaluated. Short sentence-initial words can receive implausible
alignments. The notebook's affected-word/clip counts have unclear denominators
and are not used as a prevalence estimate here.

\paragraph{Audit selection and adaptive thresholds.}
Small score-stratified audits guided threshold choice on development samples.
Listener multiplicity and inter-rater reliability are undocumented; independent
held-out validation is required to estimate policy error. Zero observed errors
in selected samples do not establish zero population error.

\paragraph{Concentration and coverage.}
Channel and cluster shares are highly unequal. Genre, accent, demographic and
lexical coverage have not been systematically characterized. Most source files
are similarly encoded AAC, with a smaller MP3 component. Boilerplate mining
misses variable-slot templates. Speaker labels and duplicate heuristics are
unvalidated, and dev/test transcripts are not manually verified.

\section{Ethics, rights and access}
\label{sec:ethics}
The material was collected from public YouTube audiobook and spoken-article
uploads. Public accessibility alone does not document permission to redistribute
the underlying audio or literary texts. The public dataset page declares no
dataset license and states that recording rights remain with their holders;
this manuscript consequently does not claim a license for third-party
recordings or unrestricted commercial reuse. Source-by-source redistribution rights and narrator consent have not been
documented here, so unrestricted reuse cannot be established.

Voices can identify narrators even when cluster IDs are pseudonymous. The
transcripts have not been manually screened for personal or sensitive content.
The public card offers a takedown route through repository issues or dataset
discussions. The paper does not establish that takedown substitutes for
permission or that voice-cloning uses have been authorized. A final resource
statement should distinguish recording rights, annotation/code licensing,
access conditions, removal procedure and intended or restricted uses.

\section{Conclusion}
\textsc{Kiraat} documents 3,105.7 hours of Turkish read speech and a versioned
policy selecting 2,575.2 hours while retaining all generated clips. The reported
segmentation comparison shows lower transcript-proxy violation rates for the
sentence-based strategy than for the implemented VAD baseline. Exploratory
listening informed removal of two candidate thresholds. These observations
support auditable data construction; they do not establish acoustic error
rates, verified speaker identities or better TTS output. Human reference
annotations, archived experimental artifacts and resolved rights/access
statements remain necessary to strengthen the resource release.
